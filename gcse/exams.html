Both papers are sat at the end of the course. There is no coursework, but you must be given the chance to do practical programming during the course, and Paper 2 tests those skills directly.

Section B of Paper 2

Section B questions ask you to write, complete, test and refine code. You must answer them in OCR Exam Reference Language or a high-level programming language such as Python. Flowcharts and plain English are not accepted in Section B. In Section A, any clear method (pseudocode, flowchart, Python) is fine unless the question says otherwise.

■ WHAT THE NUMBERS TELL YOU

  • Only 30% of marks are for recall. Most marks come from applying what you know to a scenario, and from writing and fixing code.
  • Paper 2 is where most AO3 marks are: practise writing code on paper, without an IDE.
  • Learning definitions is the start, not the finish. Practise using them in a context.

Cover the headings the question gives you, usually some of: ethical, legal, cultural, environmental, privacy. See the 8-mark question on the 1.6 page for a worked example.

Calculation questions (Paper 1)

  • No calculator. Practise multiplying and dividing by 8, 1000 and 1024 by hand.
  • Write the formula first, then substitute the numbers. Method marks are available.
  • Give units and say which unit your answer is in (bits, bytes, KB, MB).
  • OCR accepts either 1000 or 1024 for unit conversions. Be consistent and show which you used.

Code questions (Paper 2)

  • Trace, then answer. Draw a trace table before writing an output.
  • Indent consistently and close every block (endif, next i, endwhile in Exam Reference Language).
  • Small errors still cost marks. Check == vs =, brackets, and casting input() to an integer.
  • Answer what is asked. 'Identify the line' wants a line number. 'Correct the error' wants the fixed line of code.
  • Use the variable names in the question. If the question says score, don't call it s.

✗ COMMON MISTAKE: LOSING EASY MARKS

  • Writing "it is faster" without saying what is faster or why.
  • Giving the same point twice in different words for an "explain two" question.
  • Forgetting units, or mixing up bits (b) and bytes (B).
  • Writing English instead of code in Paper 2 Section B.
  • Leaving the 8-mark question until the last two minutes.

A4 Information for candidates

GCSE exams follow the Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ) rules for conducting exams. This is a summary written for this guide. Your school will display the official JCQ notices, and your invigilator's instructions always take priority.

BEFORE THE EXAM

  • Check your timetable: date, time, room and seat number.
  • Arrive at least 15 minutes early.
  • Bring black pens, a pencil, a ruler and an eraser in a clear pencil case.
  • Remember: no calculator in either Computer Science paper.

ITEMS NOT ALLOWED

  • Mobile phones, smartwatches, earbuds, or any device that can store or send information, even if switched off.
  • Calculators, notes, revision guides and paper of your own.
  • Correction fluid or tape, erasable pens, highlighters and gel pens in your answers.

DURING THE EXAM

  • Don't talk to, signal to or disturb other candidates.
  • Read the instructions on the front of the paper.
  • Write in black ink. Draw logic diagrams in pencil if you like, but go over the final version in pen.
  • If you need more space, ask for extra paper and say on the question where the rest of your answer is.

MALPRACTICE

  • Cheating, copying, having a phone on you, or helping others is malpractice.
  • Penalties range from losing marks to disqualification from the qualification.
  • Don't share or discuss the paper online after the exam.

★ EXAM TIP: ERL OR PYTHON?

  • Either is fine in Section B. Pick one and stick to it within an answer.
  • Examiners don't penalise small syntax slips if the logic is clear, but your code must be unambiguous: indent, and close blocks in ERL.
  • Arrays and strings in ERL start at index 0, just like Python.
