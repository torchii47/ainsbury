1.6.1 Impacts on society

When you discuss a technology, think about the stakeholders: everyone affected by it, e.g. users, employees, the company, competitors, the local community, governments.

AreaQuestions to askExamples
EthicalIs it right or wrong? Is it fair? Who benefits and who loses?Automation and AI replacing jobs; AI decisions that are biased because of biased training data; self-driving cars deciding in a crash; using people's data without them really understanding; monitoring employees; who is responsible when software fails
LegalDoes it break the law? Is the law keeping up?Hacking, piracy, data breaches, illegal downloads; laws differ between countries, so content hosted abroad is hard to control
CulturalHow does it change the way people live, work and communicate?The digital divide (people without devices, skills or a good connection miss out on services, jobs and education); social media changing friendships and news; online shopping and the decline of high streets; working from home; screen addiction; cyberbullying
EnvironmentalWhat is the effect on the planet?Negative: energy used by devices and data centres; mining rare metals; e-waste, often shipped abroad and dumped, leaking toxic chemicals; short device lifespans and upgrades. Positive: video calls and home working cut travel; smart heating and lighting save energy; paperless offices; modelling climate change
PrivacyWho can collect, see and share information about people?Websites and apps tracking browsing and location; cookies; CCTV and facial recognition; smart speakers listening; companies selling data; data breaches

The digital divide

The digital divide is the gap between people who have access to digital technology and the Internet, and the skills to use it, and those who don't. Causes: cost of devices and broadband; poor coverage in rural areas; age; disability; lack of skills or confidence. Effects: harder to apply for jobs, access banking and government services, do homework, see a GP online, or get the cheapest prices.

★ EXAM TIP: ALWAYS GIVE BOTH SIDES

Almost every technology has positive and negative impacts. A self-service checkout cuts queues and staff costs (positive for the shop and many customers) but can cause job losses and is harder for some older or disabled customers (negative). The examiner rewards balance.

Cultural issue

E-waste

1.6.1 Legislation

Data Protection Act 2018

Controls how personal data (data about a living, identifiable person) is collected, stored and used by organisations. It is the UK's version of the GDPR and is enforced by the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), which can issue large fines.

Principle: personal data must be...Meaning
Used fairly, lawfully and transparentlyPeople must be told what data is collected and why; there must be a legal reason, e.g. consent
Used for specified, explicit purposesOnly used for the reason it was collected
Adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessaryDon't collect more than you need
Accurate and kept up to dateErrors must be corrected
Kept no longer than necessaryDeleted when it is no longer needed
Handled securelyProtected against unauthorised access, loss or damage

Data subjects (the people the data is about) have rights, including the right to see the data held about them (a subject access request), to have it corrected, and in some cases to have it deleted.

Computer Misuse Act 1990

Makes hacking and other misuse of computers illegal. It has three main offences:

OffenceExample
1. Unauthorised access to computer materialLogging in with someone else's password to look at their files
2. Unauthorised access with intent to commit or facilitate further offencesHacking into a bank account in order to steal money
3. Unauthorised acts with intent to impair the operation of a computer (or with recklessness)Spreading a virus, deleting files, a denial of service attack

Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988

Protects the intellectual property of people who create original work: software, music, films, images, books and website content. Copyright is automatic: it doesn't need to be registered. It is illegal to copy, distribute, modify, sell or share copyrighted work without the owner's permission, e.g. pirated software, illegally streamed films, using someone's photo on your website without a licence. Patents protect inventions; they must be applied for.

Which law has been broken?

A student guesses a teacher's password and changes their own grades.

A website sells copies of a video game without the publisher's permission.

A shop sells its customers' email addresses to another company without telling them.

A gym keeps the bank details of members who left ten years ago, on an unencrypted laptop.

Someone launches a denial of service attack on a council website.

A band uses another artist's song in their video without a licence.

1.6.1 Software licences

Open sourceProprietary (closed source)
Source codeAvailable to everyone; users can view, modify and share itNot available; only the compiled program is supplied; users can't modify it
CostUsually freeUsually paid for, e.g. a one-off licence or subscription
LicenceAllows changes and redistribution (sometimes changed versions must also be open source)Restricts how many people or devices can use it; copying is illegal

★ EXAM TIP: CHOOSE FOR THE SCENARIO

A charity with no money and a volunteer who can program might choose open source: free, and they can modify it. A hospital that needs guaranteed support and reliability might choose proprietary. Justify with the scenario.

Open source software
Software whose source code is freely available for anyone to view, modify and redistribute.
Proprietary software
Software whose source code is kept private by the owner; users buy a licence to use it but can't modify or share it.
Source code
The original program written by the programmer in a programming language.

The 8-mark question

Paper 1 usually ends with an extended response question worth 8 marks, marked in levels. It often asks you to discuss the impact of a technology, using headings such as ethical, legal, cultural, environmental and privacy.

LevelMarksWhat it looks like
36–8Thorough knowledge, applied to the context throughout. Well-developed points covering several areas and both sides. Logical, with a conclusion.
23–5Reasonable knowledge, some application to the context. Some points developed. Some balance.
11–2Basic, mostly generic points. Often a list. Little development.

Worked example

Question: A city council plans to install smart cameras with facial recognition in the town centre. Discuss the ethical, legal, privacy and cultural issues of this plan. (8 marks)

Decide which level each answer reaches before you reveal the examiner's comment.

Answer 1: It will catch criminals. It invades privacy. It might be illegal. People might not like it. It costs a lot of money.

Level 1, 2 marks. A list of points. Nothing is explained or linked to the town centre. There's no development of either side.

Answer 2: Facial recognition could help the police find wanted criminals quickly, which makes the town centre safer. However, it records everyone, not just criminals, which is an invasion of privacy. The council must follow the Data Protection Act, so it must keep the images secure and not keep them too long. Some people may avoid the town centre because they feel watched.

Level 2, 4 marks. Points are explained and both sides are considered, with a relevant law. Covers privacy, legal and cultural points, but the ethical issue isn't developed and there's no conclusion.

Answer 3: Ethically, the cameras could make the town centre safer: police could find missing people and wanted criminals faster, which protects shoppers. But facial recognition has been shown to misidentify some groups more than others, so innocent people could be stopped unfairly, which is discrimination. Legally, faces are biometric personal data, so under the Data Protection Act 2018 the council must have a lawful reason, tell people, store the data securely and delete it when it's no longer needed; a data breach could lead to large fines from the ICO. Privacy is the biggest concern: everyone is tracked, not just suspects, and people's movements could be built into a profile without their consent. Culturally, people may change how they behave in public, such as avoiding protests, and some may shop elsewhere, hurting local businesses; others may feel safer and visit more. Overall, the cameras could improve safety, but only if strict rules limit how long data is kept and who can access it; without these, the loss of privacy for everyone outweighs the benefits.

Level 3, 8 marks. Every heading covered, with well-developed chains of reasoning applied to the town centre. Both sides, a relevant law used accurately, and a supported conclusion.

Have a go

A supermarket chain is replacing most of its staffed checkouts with self-service checkouts and an app that lets customers scan items as they shop. Discuss the impact of this change. In your answer you should consider: ethical issues, cultural issues, environmental issues, privacy issues.

◆ SUPPORT: SENTENCE STARTERS

  • One ethical issue is ... because ... This means that ...
  • On the other hand, customers who ...
  • The app could collect ... which raises privacy concerns because ...
  • Overall, I think ... because ...

Indicative content:

  • Ethical: checkout staff may lose jobs or have hours cut; staff may be retrained to help customers; lower costs could mean lower prices; is it fair to make customers do the work?
  • Cultural: less social contact, which matters to some older or lonely customers; digital divide: people without smartphones or digital skills, or with some disabilities, find it harder; faster for confident users; changes expectations of shopping.
  • Environmental: fewer printed receipts if the app sends e-receipts; more devices to manufacture, power and eventually dispose of (e-waste); customers' phones use energy.
  • Privacy: the app can record everything a customer buys, when and where; data may be used for targeted advertising or sold; must follow the Data Protection Act 2018; risk of data breach.
  • Conclusion: a supported judgement, e.g. beneficial if staffed tills remain for those who need them and data use is transparent.

Exam-style questions

Allow about 25 minutes. Answer each question before you look at its mark scheme.

Describe what is meant by the 'digital divide'.

  • The gap between people who have access to technology/the Internet (1) and those who don't, e.g. because of cost, location or skills (1).

Give three principles of the Data Protection Act 2018.

  • Any three: used fairly, lawfully and transparently; used for specified purposes only; adequate, relevant and limited/not excessive; accurate and up to date; not kept longer than necessary; kept secure.

A student uses a teacher's login, which they saw being typed, to look at the next day's test. State which law they have broken and which offence.

  • Computer Misuse Act 1990 (1).
  • Unauthorised access to computer material (1). (Accept: with intent to commit further offence if justified, e.g. cheating.)

Explain how the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 protects a games developer.

  • It makes it illegal to copy/distribute/sell the game without permission (1) so the developer gets paid for their work / others can't profit from it (1).

A school is choosing between open source and proprietary office software. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of choosing open source software.

  • Advantage: usually free (1) so the school saves money for other resources / can install it on every computer (1). Accept: can be modified to suit the school.
  • Disadvantage: no official support (1) so IT staff may struggle to fix problems / rely on forums (1). Accept: may not be compatible with files from proprietary software.

Describe two environmental impacts of people upgrading their smartphones every year.

  • E-waste: old phones thrown away (1) may be dumped/shipped abroad and release toxic chemicals (1).
  • Manufacturing new phones uses rare metals/raw materials (1) which must be mined, damaging habitats / using energy (1).

A delivery company is planning to use drones instead of vans to deliver small parcels. Discuss the ethical, legal, cultural and environmental issues of this plan.

Indicative content:

  • Ethical: delivery drivers lose jobs; new jobs for drone operators/engineers; who is responsible if a drone crashes and injures someone?
  • Legal: drones carry cameras, so footage of people/homes is personal data under the Data Protection Act; airspace and flight rules; liability for damage.
  • Cultural: faster deliveries change how people shop; noise and drones overhead may be unwelcome; people in rural areas may get better access; digital divide if booking needs an app.
  • Environmental: electric drones produce less pollution than vans on short trips; batteries need materials and disposal; noise disturbs wildlife.
  • Conclusion weighing up the issues.
