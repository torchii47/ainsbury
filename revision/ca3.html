Content Area 3: Emerging Issues

Content area 3 is about how digital technology changes the way organisations work, the way society behaves, and who gets left behind. Exam questions are often set in a scenario and ask you to discuss or evaluate impacts. The marks come from applying the points to the context and weighing up both sides.

Links to other content areas: accessibility regulations and bias link to Content area 4 (Legislation: equality and guidelines such as WCAG). Bias as a business risk links to Content area 5 (5.3 audience exclusion).

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE: APPLYING TO A CONTEXT

  • Scenario: a Leeds-based insurance company moves its call centre staff to hybrid working.
  • Point: staff no longer commute every day. Linked: this saves them time and money, which may improve staff retention.
  • Counterpoint: new staff miss out on learning by listening to experienced colleagues. Linked: this could lengthen training and increase mistakes on claims.
  • Notice that each point names the context (call centre, claims, commute) rather than speaking generally.

3.1.2 Digital inclusion

Digital inclusion means making sure everyone has fair access to digital services. If a service is only available online, anyone who cannot use it is shut out of something they may need, such as a GP appointment or benefits claim.

FactorWhat it meansHow organisations can help
Suitable technologies (hardware and software)People need a device that can run the service and software that works with it.Design for low-spec phones and older browsers; offer device lending (e.g. libraries); support assistive tech such as screen readers.
ConnectivityPeople need reliable, affordable internet. Rural areas may have poor broadband or mobile signal.Make pages lightweight; allow offline use; provide public Wi-Fi; offer alternative channels (phone, in person).
Checking for bias within datasetsIf training data under-represents a group, the system may work badly for them, e.g. facial recognition less accurate for darker skin tones.Audit datasets; test with diverse users; monitor outcomes by group; keep humans involved in important decisions.
Conforming to codes of best practiceFollowing recognised guidance such as WCAG and the GOV.UK Service Standard.Build accessibility into design from the start; test with real users including disabled users.
Public sector accessibility regulationsThe Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) Accessibility Regulations 2018.See below.

Public sector bodies' website and mobile applications accessibility regulations

Purpose: to make sure public sector websites and apps (councils, NHS, schools, colleges, government departments) can be used by as many people as possible, including disabled people.

Key features:

  • Websites and apps must meet the WCAG accessibility standard (level AA).
  • They must publish an accessibility statement: how accessible the service is, which parts are not, and how users can report problems or request content in another format.
  • They must be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust (the four WCAG principles).
  • Compliance is monitored by the Government Digital Service; enforcement is through equality law.
WCAG principleMeaningExample
PerceivableUsers can see or hear the contentAlt text on images; captions on videos; good colour contrast
OperableUsers can navigate and use itWorks with a keyboard only; no time limits that cannot be extended
UnderstandableContent and controls make sensePlain language; clear error messages on forms; consistent navigation
RobustWorks with different technologiesValid code that works with screen readers and different browsers

3.2.1 Emerging technologies

For each technology, know what it is, give examples, and explain its impact on organisations, individuals and society, both positive and negative.

Storage media: increased demand for storage

  • More data than ever is created: 4K video, IoT sensors, cloud backups, AI training data, social media.
  • Organisations: higher costs for storage and data centres; need data management and retention policies (link to data protection: don't keep data longer than needed).
  • Society: data centres use large amounts of electricity and water for cooling.
  • Response: higher-capacity SSDs and HDDs, cloud storage, compression, archiving cold data to cheaper storage such as tape.

Processing technologies: quantum computing

  • Uses qubits, which can be in a combination of 0 and 1 at once (superposition), so certain calculations can be done far faster than on conventional computers.
  • Potential uses: drug discovery and chemistry simulation, optimising logistics routes, financial modelling, weather and climate modelling.
  • Risk: could break current public-key encryption (e.g. RSA), threatening online banking and secure communication. Organisations are preparing 'post-quantum' encryption.
  • Currently very expensive, needs extreme cooling, error-prone, and available only to large organisations and via the cloud.

Edge computing processes data on or near the device instead of sending everything to the cloud. Benefits: faster response (lower latency), important for self-driving cars and factory safety systems; less bandwidth used; data can stay local for privacy. Drawbacks: more devices to secure and maintain; less processing power than a data centre.

Open source software

  • Examples: Linux, Firefox, LibreOffice, Python, VLC, WordPress.
  • Benefits: free to use; code can be checked and modified; large communities fix bugs quickly; avoids lock-in to one vendor.
  • Drawbacks: no guaranteed support; quality and documentation vary; projects can be abandoned; vulnerabilities in widely used open source libraries can affect thousands of organisations at once; licences have conditions (some require you to share your changes).

Blockchain

  • A decentralised ledger: copies are held by many computers, and each block is cryptographically linked to the previous one, so records cannot be secretly changed.
  • Uses: cryptocurrency (Bitcoin), supply chain tracking (proving where food or diamonds came from), smart contracts, digital certificates.
  • Benefits: transparency; tamper resistance; no single point of failure; no need for a trusted middleman.
  • Drawbacks: some blockchains use huge amounts of energy; slow compared with a normal database; data cannot be deleted (conflicts with the right to erasure); cryptocurrency linked to scams and price volatility.

3.2.2 Making judgements about impact

Extended questions ask you to weigh up the impact of a technology on organisations, society and individuals in a given scenario. Use this structure:

  1. Identify the technology and who is affected in the scenario.
  2. Positive impacts, each with a linked explanation tied to the context.
  3. Negative impacts, each with a linked explanation tied to the context.
  4. Interrelationships: how one impact leads to another. For example, IoT sensors create more data, which increases storage demand, which raises energy use.
  5. Judgement (for evaluate questions): which side is stronger, and under what conditions? 'The benefits outweigh the drawbacks provided that...'

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE: A SUPERMARKET INTRODUCING AI-POWERED SELF-CHECKOUTS WITH AGE ESTIMATION

  • Organisation: fewer staff needed on tills, so lower costs; but up-front cost, and staff still needed to help when machines fail.
  • Customers: faster checkout for many; but older customers or those with low digital skills may find it harder, a digital inclusion issue.
  • Society: fewer entry-level jobs; shift towards technical roles maintaining machines.
  • Bias: age estimation trained on unrepresentative data could misjudge some ethnic groups, leading to unfair refusals.
  • Interrelationship: if customers are wrongly refused, the store gets reputational damage, which links to business risk (CA5).
  • Judgement: worthwhile for a large store if staffed tills remain and the AI is tested for bias, because this keeps the cost savings without excluding customers.

Exam-style questions

Allow about 75 minutes.

State two changes in communication methods caused by increased reliance on digital systems in the workplace.

  • Any two: more email instead of letters or face-to-face; video calls instead of in-person meetings; instant messaging/chat tools; collaboration platforms (e.g. Teams). One mark each.

Explain one benefit and one drawback of staff monitoring software for a call centre.

  • Benefit (1) with linked explanation (1), e.g. calls can be reviewed (1) so managers can identify training needs and improve customer service (1).
  • Drawback (1) with linked explanation (1), e.g. staff may feel distrusted (1) which can lower morale and increase staff turnover (1).
  • Accept: protecting data security; evidence in complaints; privacy concerns; stress.

Define the term 'digital footprint'.

  • The trail of data a person leaves through their online activity (1).

Describe the purpose of the public sector bodies' websites and mobile applications accessibility regulations.

  • To make sure public sector websites and apps are accessible to as many people as possible, including disabled people (1).
  • By requiring them to meet accessibility standards (WCAG AA) and publish an accessibility statement (1).

A council is redesigning its online form for reporting missed bin collections. Explain how two end user characteristics should affect the design.

  • Characteristic (1) + linked design effect (1), twice. E.g.:
  • Age: older residents may have poorer eyesight (1) so text should be resizable and high-contrast (1).
  • Literacy/language: some residents may have low literacy (1) so use plain English and clear icons with labels (1).
  • Additional needs: blind users use screen readers (1) so form fields need proper labels (1).
  • Digital skills: low skills (1) so keep the form short with step-by-step guidance (1).

Explain what is meant by edge computing and why it is used in self-driving cars.

  • Processing data on or near the device where it is generated rather than in a central cloud (1).
  • Self-driving cars must react in milliseconds (1).
  • Sending data to the cloud and waiting for a response would add latency, which could cause an accident / connection may drop (1).

Give two benefits to a software developer of continuing professional development.

  • Any two: keeps skills up to date with new technologies; improves employability/job security; supports promotion; awareness of and adherence to industry standards; increased competence.

Explain why checking for bias in datasets is important when a bank uses machine learning to approve loans.

  • If historical data reflects past discrimination or under-represents some groups (1)…
  • …the model will learn those patterns and unfairly reject applicants from those groups (1)…
  • …which could breach equality law / damage the bank's reputation / exclude customers (1).

Explain two environmental impacts of a company replacing all staff laptops every two years.

  • Impact (1) + explanation (1), twice. E.g.:
  • Uses rare metals (1); mining them damages environments and supplies are limited (1).
  • Energy used to manufacture new laptops (1) increases the company's carbon footprint (1).
  • Old laptops become e-waste (1); toxic materials can pollute if not recycled properly (1).

A small charity is deciding whether to use open source software instead of paid software. Explain one benefit and one drawback.

  • Benefit (1) + explanation (1): free, so budget can go to the charity's services (1).
  • Drawback (1) + explanation (1): no guaranteed support, so if something breaks a volunteer may not be able to fix it, disrupting work (1).
  • Accept other valid points: community support, customisable, compatibility issues, staff unfamiliar with it.

A marketing agency is considering using generative AI to produce social media content for clients. Discuss the impact on the agency, its staff and its clients.

Indicative content:

  • Agency: produce content faster and cheaper; take on more clients; compete on price. But risk of inaccurate or off-brand content; copyright uncertainty over AI output; reputational damage if poor quality content is published.
  • Staff: routine writing reduced, freeing time for strategy and creativity; new skills needed (prompting, editing AI output). But fear of job losses; junior roles may disappear, reducing training routes.
  • Clients: lower costs and faster turnaround. But may not want AI content; concerns about confidential product information being entered into AI tools; need for transparency.
  • Society: more AI content online; misinformation risk.
  • Discuss: no conclusion required, but balanced consideration of both sides needed for Level 3.

A city council plans to install IoT sensors on street lights to monitor traffic, air quality and pedestrian numbers. Evaluate the impact of this plan on the council and residents.

◆ SUPPORT: SENTENCE STARTERS

  • One benefit for the agency would be ... because ...
  • However, staff might ... which could lead to ...
  • Clients may be concerned that ...
  • The sensors would allow the council to ... This means that ...
  • On the other hand, residents may worry about ...
  • Overall, I think ... because ...

Indicative content:

  • Council benefits: real-time traffic data allows signals to be adjusted, reducing congestion; air quality data targets pollution hotspots; evidence for planning decisions; possible cost savings (e.g. lights dim when no pedestrians).
  • Council drawbacks: high installation and maintenance cost; large volumes of data to store and secure; devices could be hacked; must comply with data protection law; reliance on suppliers.
  • Residents benefits: cleaner air, safer streets, shorter journeys; better-informed services.
  • Residents drawbacks: privacy concerns about surveillance, especially if cameras are used; loss of trust; benefits may not reach all areas equally.
  • Interrelationships: more data increases storage demand and energy use; security of IoT devices affects whether benefits are realised.
  • Judgement: e.g. worthwhile if sensors collect anonymised data, are secured properly and the council is transparent with residents.

▲ STRETCH

  • For question 12, consider the link between the sensors, data storage demands and the council's obligations under data protection law (CA4).
  • Explain how quantum computing could affect the security of the data collected by the sensors in the future.
