Content Area 5: Business Context

Content area 5 looks at the organisations that digital systems are built for: what they do, who has an interest in them, how digital systems add value, what can go wrong, and how change is managed. Paper 2 questions are often based on a short scenario about a business. Use the details in the scenario in every answer.

Links to other content areas: risks link to Content area 8 (Security) and Content area 4 (Legislation). Implementation testing links to Content area 2 (2.12 Testing). Backups and resilience link to Content area 7 (7.6).

Potential impact of risks (5.3.2)

ImpactExplanation
Legal actionCustomers, staff or partners sue for damages; regulators prosecute.
FinesE.g. the ICO can fine up to £17.5 million or 4% of global turnover for data protection breaches.
Reputational damageBad publicity; customers lose trust and go elsewhere; harder to recruit.

★ EXAM TIP: CHAIN THE RISK TO THE IMPACT

A risk on its own is not a full answer. Link it: 'A security breach exposing patient records (risk) would breach data protection law, so the clinic could be fined by the ICO (impact), and patients may move to another practice (impact).'

Confidentiality, integrity, availability
The three security properties that a breach can compromise (the CIA triad).
Audience exclusion
When some users cannot use or are disadvantaged by a system, e.g. through bias or poor user experience.

5.4.4 The change management process

Change management is the controlled process for making changes to digital systems so that they are planned, approved, tested and documented, and so disruption is kept to a minimum.

StageWhat happens
Identify the type of changeNew system, or amendment to an existing system?
Role of the change advisory board (CAB)Prioritise change requests; review them; manage stages of approval; monitor the change (collate and analyse change data, check implementation, take action to accelerate change); provide feedback.
Identify the changes to be madeSet SMARTER objectives (below).
Identify impact of changeMeasure or forecast positive and negative impact; analyse it.
Allocation of resourcesBudget, time, staffing, hardware and software.
Communicate risks and desired impact to stakeholdersTo gain acceptance and ensure compliance.
Configuration of the new system or processIntegration with legacy systems; maintaining service during the change.
Fully test the new systemResults must be reproducible; use a test environment (hardware and software) that matches the live one.
Choose the implementation methodParallel, phased, direct or pilot (below).
Document the change processRequirement traceability (responsibility and accountability); up-to-date information; record all decisions; retain change documentation; user training manuals.
Rollback planningBackup methodology, backup location, recovery plan, so the old system can be restored if the change fails.
Identify training needsNew training requirements; refresher courses.
Monitor progressPost-progress review: did the change meet its objectives?
Version control softwareTracks every change to code and documents so earlier versions can be restored and changes traced to a person.

SMARTER objectives

LetterMeaningExample: new online booking system for a dental practice

MMeasurable40% of bookings made online
AAchievableUses an existing booking platform the practice can afford
RRealisticStaff have time to be trained
TTime-boundLive within 3 months

Implementation methods

MethodHow it worksBenefitsDrawbacksBest for
ParallelOld and new systems run side by side; results compared.Lowest risk: old system is a fallback; outputs can be checked.Double the work and cost; staff may resist dropping old system.Critical systems: payroll, banking
PhasedNew system introduced one module at a time.Problems limited to one part; staff learn gradually.Slow; old and new must work together during transition.Large systems with separate modules
DirectOld system switched off, new one switched on at once.Quickest and cheapest; no duplication.Highest risk: if it fails there is no fallback.Small or non-critical systems; when old system cannot continue
PilotNew system used in one branch or by one group first.Problems found on a small scale; pilot users can train others.Slower to roll out everywhere; pilot site bears the risk.Organisations with many similar branches

✎ WORKED EXAMPLE

A supermarket chain with 300 stores is replacing its till software. Pilot is suitable: install in three stores first, fix problems with real customers on a small scale, then roll out. Direct would risk every store being unable to take payments at once; parallel is impractical because each till can only run one system.

Practise: which implementation method suits best?

A council's payroll system, where every payment must be checked against the old system for a month.

A bank with 80 branches trialling new counter software.

A small café replacing a broken booking spreadsheet that can no longer be opened.

A hospital moving to a new system one module at a time: appointments first, then prescriptions, then billing.

Change advisory board (CAB)
A group that reviews, prioritises and approves proposed changes to systems and monitors their implementation.
SMARTER
Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound, Evaluated, Reviewed: a framework for setting objectives.
Version control
Software that tracks every change to files, who made it and when, so earlier versions can be restored (e.g. Git).
Requirement traceability
Being able to link each change back to the requirement it meets and who was responsible.
Rollback
Returning to the previous version of a system if a change fails.
Pilot implementation
Introducing the new system in one location or to one group first, then rolling it out.
Direct implementation
Switching off the old system and starting the new one at the same moment (big bang).
Phased implementation
Introducing the new system one part (module) at a time.
Parallel implementation
Running the old and new systems side by side for a period.

5.4.5 Feasibility of a digital project

A feasibility study decides whether a project is worth doing. It weighs benefits against drawbacks, risks and constraints.

FactorExamples
Benefits and drawbacks
  • Financial savings (e.g. fewer staff hours)
  • Cost of implementing change (hardware, software, training, consultants)
  • Impact on processes: productivity gains, improved communication, security
  • Provision of new products
  • Impact on company reputation
Risks
  • Resistance to change from the workforce
  • Misuse of new systems
  • Inadequate support for the new system
  • Inadequate knowledge of the new system
  • Disruption caused by implementation
Constraints
  • Budget
  • Time
  • Human resources and technological resources

★ EXAM TIP

In an evaluate question about feasibility, weigh the benefits against costs, risks and constraints for the organisation in the scenario, then make a judgement: is it feasible, and under what conditions? For example: 'It is feasible if implemented in phases so the cost is spread over two financial years.'

Feasibility
Whether a project is practical and worthwhile given its benefits, risks and constraints.

Exam-style questions

Allow about 80 minutes.

State the difference between the public sector and the private sector.

  • Public sector organisations are owned/funded by government to provide services (1); private sector organisations are owned by individuals/shareholders and usually aim to make a profit (1).

Give one example of an NGO.

  • Any valid NGO, e.g. Oxfam, Amnesty International, Greenpeace, Red Cross.

Identify two internal stakeholders and two external stakeholders of a college.

  • Internal (any two): owners/governors; directors/principal/senior leaders; employees/lecturers/support staff.
  • External (any two): students/customers; parents; suppliers; government/funding bodies (e.g. DfE); employers/partners; Ofsted. One mark each.

Explain how real-time monitoring of KPIs could help the manager of a parcel delivery company.

  • Manager can see where vans are / parcels delivered in real time (1) so problems such as delays can be spotted and resources redeployed quickly (1).

Explain two ways a digital system can reduce pain points for users of a train ticket app.

  • Pain point (1) + how the system reduces it (1), twice. E.g. response time: show live delay information and estimated times (1) so passengers can plan (1). Complexity: save payment details and favourite journeys (1) so buying takes a few taps (1).

A hospital's patient record system fails for six hours. Explain two potential impacts on the hospital.

  • Impact (1) + explanation (1), twice. E.g. patient safety risk because staff cannot see medication history (1), possible legal action (1); reputational damage (1) loss of public trust (1); disruption/cancelled appointments (1); regulatory investigation (1).

Explain one political and one technological factor that could trigger change in a bank.

  • Political (1) + explanation (1): e.g. new government regulations on open banking require the bank to share data via APIs, so systems must change (1).
  • Technological (1) + explanation (1): e.g. a zero-day vulnerability in online banking software requires urgent patching (1); customers expect new technology such as biometric login (1).

Describe the role of a change advisory board.

  • Any three: reviews change requests; prioritises them; approves or rejects through stages of approval; monitors the change (collating data, checking implementation, accelerating); provides feedback.

■ SCENARIO FOR QUESTIONS 9–13

BrewBox is a Yorkshire SME with 60 staff that sells coffee subscriptions online to customers and supplies coffee beans to cafés. It uses an old stock system and a separate website. The directors want a new integrated system covering the website, stock, deliveries and customer accounts.

State which business model BrewBox uses and justify your answer.

  • B2M (1): it sells subscriptions to individual customers (B2C) and supplies cafés (B2B) (1). Accept B2C or B2B only if clearly justified with reference to one part (max 1).

Write two SMARTER objectives for BrewBox's new system.

  • One mark per objective that is specific and measurable with a time element, e.g. 'All stock levels update automatically when an online order is placed, from launch in March.' 'Reduce delivery errors by 50% within six months.'

Explain why BrewBox needs a rollback plan.

  • If the new system fails or has serious bugs (1) BrewBox could restore the old system and data from backup (1) so customers can still order and income is not lost (1).

Discuss the suitability of direct and parallel implementation for BrewBox's new system.

Indicative content:

  • Direct: cheapest and quickest, important for an SME; no need to run two systems. But high risk: if the new system fails, customers cannot order and cafés cannot be supplied; no fallback.
  • Parallel: low risk because the old system continues; outputs can be compared (e.g. stock levels). But 60 staff would have to enter data twice; costly and time-consuming; the old website and stock system are separate, making parallel running complex.
  • Could consider timing a direct change during a quiet period, with a strong rollback plan, as a compromise.

Evaluate the feasibility of BrewBox's new integrated system.

◆ SUPPORT: SENTENCE STARTERS

  • Direct implementation would be quicker because ... however, if the system failed ...
  • Parallel implementation means staff would have to ...
  • BrewBox would benefit from the new system because ...
  • A risk is that staff ... which could mean ...
  • BrewBox is an SME, so the budget ...

Indicative content:

  • Benefits: integrated stock and website avoids selling out-of-stock items; automated deliveries; customer accounts improve retention; better reporting for directors; supports growth in both B2C and B2B.
  • Costs: software, hardware, consultants, training, data migration from old system; significant for an SME.
  • Risks: staff resistance; inadequate knowledge of the new system; disruption during implementation; integration problems with the legacy stock data.
  • Constraints: limited budget and IT staff in an SME; time (avoid busy periods such as Christmas).
  • Judgement: e.g. feasible if phased over time to spread cost, with training and a rollback plan, because the long-term savings and customer benefits outweigh the costs.

Explain the importance of version control software during a change to a company's website.

  • Point (1) + linked explanation (1), twice. E.g. tracks every change and who made it (1) so mistakes can be traced and responsibility identified (1); allows rollback to a previous working version (1) if the update breaks the site (1); allows several developers to work at once without overwriting each other's work (1)(1).

Explain why documentation is important in the change management process.

  • Point (1) + linked explanation (1), twice. E.g. requirement traceability (1) so every change can be linked to a requirement and a responsible person (1); records decisions (1) so the reasons can be reviewed later (1); user training manuals (1) help staff use the new system (1); keeps information up to date for future maintenance (1)(1).
