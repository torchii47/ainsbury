Pre-release material

MOORSIDE BIKE HIRE

Moorside Bike Hire is a small community interest company in a fictional Yorkshire market town. It hires standard and electric bikes to visitors and local people. All of its profit pays for free cycling lessons for local children.

It has three hire points: Station, Castle and Riverside. There is one manager, Priya, and six part-time staff, four of whom are volunteers. Some volunteers are over 70 and aren't confident with computers.

The problem

Each hire is written on a paper form. Priya collects the forms every Friday and types the totals into a spreadsheet. This causes problems:

  • Staff can't see how many bikes are free at another hire point, so customers are sometimes sent to a site with none left.
  • Prices are worked out by hand, and mistakes happen. Last summer around 1 in 20 forms had the wrong price.
  • Forms containing customers' names and phone numbers are kept in an unlocked drawer.
  • Priya can't easily see which hire points or bike types make the most money, which she needs for funding bids.

What Moorside wants

Moorside wants a simple prototype, written in Python, that staff can use on a shared laptop at each hire point.

RefRequirement
R1Record a hire: hire point, bike type, number of hours and whether the customer is a member.
R2Work out the cost using the price list.
R3Reject invalid input with a clear message.
R4Show how many bikes are available at a hire point.
R5Report the total income for each hire point.
R6Be simple enough for a volunteer to use after a short demonstration.
R7Protect customers' personal data.

Your notes

Task 1: Planning

Produce a plan for the prototype. Your plan must include:

  1. a decomposition of the problem into smaller parts
  2. functional and non-functional requirements, linked to R1–R7
  3. three SMARTER objectives for the project
  4. a feasibility assessment, including the main risks and how you'd reduce them
  5. a justified development timeline
Example responses: requirements and objectives

Weak

The system must work well and be easy to use. It should be finished on time. It needs to be secure.

Why: these could describe any project. Nothing can be measured or tested, and Moorside isn't mentioned.

Middling

The system must calculate the cost of a hire using the price list (R2). It must stop staff entering hours outside 1 to 8 (R3). Objective: finish the prototype by the end of June.

Why: the requirements are linked to the brief and can be tested. The objective has a date, but it doesn't say what success looks like or why it matters.

Strong

Functional: calculate the correct cost for all 32 combinations of hours, bike type and membership (R2), so the 1 in 20 pricing errors are removed. Non-functional: a new volunteer can record a hire correctly within 2 minutes after a 10-minute demonstration (R6), because some volunteers lack confidence with computers. Objective: by 30 June, Station staff record every hire in the prototype for two weeks with no pricing errors, checked by Priya against a sample of 50 hires.

Why: each point is specific and measurable, and it's justified using evidence from the scenario. It also separates what the system does from how well it does it.

Strong

Line 8, logic error: multiplying by 0.1 charges members 10% of the price instead of taking 10% off, so a £10 hire cost £1. Changed to cost * 0.9. Tested with a 2-hour standard hire for a member: expected £9.00, got £9.00.

Why: it explains the effect of the defect and gives evidence from a test that the fix works.

Task 3: Design

Design the prototype. On paper or in your notes, produce:

  2. a data model showing the data to be stored, with field names and data types

Task 4b: Reflective evaluation

Evaluate your prototype. Judge how well it meets R1–R7, using your test results as evidence. Include its limitations, what you'd improve and what you'd do differently next time. Use the evaluation guide to help you.

Example responses: evaluating one requirement

Weak

I made a function that works out the price. It works well. I used if statements and it was hard at first but I fixed it.

Why: this describes what happened instead of judging it. "Works well" has no evidence, and it doesn't mention Moorside or a requirement.

Middling

R2 is met because the cost function passed my tests. A weakness is that prices are written into the code, so it would be better to store them in a file.

Why: it makes a judgement, mentions evidence and gives a weakness. It doesn't say what the tests showed or why the improvement would help Moorside.

Strong

R2 is fully met: all 14 cost tests passed, including members hiring electric bikes and the 1 and 8 hour limits, so this should remove the 1 in 20 pricing errors from the paper forms. However, the prices are written into the code, which means Priya would need a developer every time prices change. Storing them in a CSV file would let her update them herself, so the system would stay accurate without extra cost. Overall the pricing is reliable enough to trial, but this limitation should be fixed before Moorside's next price change.

Why: it uses specific evidence, links back to the problem in the brief, balances a strength and a limitation, and ends with a supported judgement.
