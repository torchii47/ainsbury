EXAM TECHNIQUE · PAPER 1 AND PAPER 2

Worked Long Answers

Three answers to the same question: weak, middling and strong. Try each question yourself first, then see what moves an answer up the levels.

A

How to use these

  1. Write your own answer, timed at about 1.5 minutes per mark.
  2. Open the example answers and decide which is closest to yours.
  3. Rewrite your answer using the notes.

These questions were written for this guide. The marks are a guide based on the levels below. They aren't official marking, and real answers can be strong in different ways.

★ WHAT MOVES AN ANSWER UP

  • Use the scenario: name the organisation and use its details in every point.
  • Develop your points: use "because", "so" and "which means" to explain the effect.
  • Show both sides: a one-sided answer can't reach the top level.
  • Finish with a judgement: for 'evaluate', say what you decide and why it outweighs the other side. See command words.

Paper 1 style: AI chatbot (12 marks)

Greenfield Housing is a housing association with 4,000 tenants, many of them elderly. Tenants phone a small team to report repairs. Greenfield plans to replace the phone line with an AI chatbot on its website, available all day and night.

Evaluate Greenfield Housing's plan to replace its phone line with an AI chatbot. [12 marks]

Show the example answers

Weak: Level 1, about 3 marks

Chatbots are good because they are available 24/7 and save money. They use AI so they are quick. However some people do not like them. There might be data protection issues. Overall it is a good idea because technology is the future.

Why: these are accurate but generic points that could apply to any business. Nothing is developed, and the judgement isn't supported.

Middling: Level 2, about 7 marks

The chatbot would help Greenfield because tenants could report repairs at any time, including at night when the phone line is closed. This would reduce calls to the small team, so they could deal with urgent problems. However, many tenants are elderly and may not have internet access or feel confident online, so they might not be able to report repairs. Greenfield would also be storing tenants' personal data, so it would have to follow the Data Protection Act 2018. Overall I think Greenfield should use the chatbot, but it should keep the phone line too.

Why: the points use the scenario and look at both sides. The legal point isn't developed, and the judgement is stated without saying why it's the best choice.

Strong: Level 3, about 11 marks

A chatbot would let Greenfield's 4,000 tenants report repairs at any hour, which means problems such as leaks could be logged at night rather than waiting for the team to open. Routine reports would also leave the small team free for complex or urgent cases, so the service could improve without hiring more staff.

However, many tenants are elderly, so removing the phone line risks digital exclusion. A tenant with no internet access couldn't report a repair at all, which could leave them in unsafe housing. As a provider of an essential service, Greenfield should also consider its duties under the Equality Act 2010. A chatbot may misunderstand unusual problems too. If it treats a gas smell as a routine repair, the result could be dangerous, so emergencies need a human.

The chatbot would collect personal data, including addresses and possibly health needs. Under the Data Protection Act 2018, Greenfield must keep this secure and use only what it needs, so it must check how any supplier stores and processes the data.

Overall, Greenfield should introduce the chatbot but not as a full replacement. The benefit of 24-hour reporting is real, but it doesn't outweigh the risk of excluding elderly tenants or mishandling emergencies. Keeping a phone line for emergencies and for tenants who need it gives most of the savings without those risks.

Why: every point is applied to Greenfield and developed to a consequence. It weighs up both sides, and the judgement explains which factor matters most.

Paper 2 style: Moving to the cloud (12 marks)

Harlow Print is a printing company with 60 staff at one site. Its customer orders and design files are on two ageing servers in a storeroom, looked after by one IT technician. Harlow Print is deciding whether to move everything to a cloud service.

Evaluate whether Harlow Print should move its systems to the cloud. [12 marks]

Show the example answers

Weak: Level 1, about 3 marks

The cloud is cheaper and you can access it anywhere. It is backed up. But you need the internet and it might not be secure. I think they should move to the cloud because most companies do now.

Why: this is a list of general features. "Most companies do" isn't a reason based on Harlow Print's situation.

Middling: Level 2, about 7 marks

Harlow Print's servers are old, so they could fail and the company could lose orders. Moving to the cloud would mean the provider looks after the hardware, so the one technician wouldn't have to. Staff could also get to design files from other places. However, design files are large, so a slow connection could cause problems, and if the internet fails staff can't work. Monthly costs could also be high. Overall, Harlow Print should move to the cloud because the servers are old.

Why: this answer is applied and balanced, but the points stop at the first effect and the conclusion only repeats one point.

Strong: Level 3, about 11 marks

Harlow Print's two ageing servers are a single point of failure. If one fails, orders could stop, and with only one technician the repair could take days. A cloud provider offers built-in redundancy and backups, which would greatly improve resilience. It also replaces a large upfront cost for new servers with a monthly fee, which spreads the cost and can grow with the business.

However, the move creates new dependencies. Design files are large, so a slow connection would delay staff every time they open or upload work, and an internet outage could stop production completely. Harlow Print would need a faster connection and probably a backup line, which adds cost. Monthly fees also continue forever, so over several years the cloud could cost more than replacing the servers.

Customer order data would be held by a third party. Harlow Print stays responsible for protecting it under the Data Protection Act 2018, so it must choose a provider carefully. Even with a cloud service, Harlow Print must still manage user accounts and permissions, so the technician's role changes rather than disappears.

Overall, Harlow Print should move to the cloud, because the risk of losing everything when an old server fails is greater than the risk of slower access, which can be reduced with a better connection. It should move orders first and test how large design files perform before moving those.

Why: it links each point to the company's real situation, considers cost over time and ends with a reasoned decision and a practical next step.

Discuss: Staff using their own phones (6 marks)

Bright Smiles is a chain of five dental practices. Its managers are thinking about letting receptionists use their own phones to read work emails, which contain patient details.

Discuss the security issues of letting Bright Smiles staff use their own phones for work email. [6 marks]

Show the example answers

Weak: Level 1, about 2 marks

Phones can get lost or stolen. They could get a virus. Staff should use a password.

Why: these are isolated, general points with no link to Bright Smiles or patient data.

Middling: Level 2, about 4 marks

If a receptionist loses their phone, someone could read emails with patient details, which would be a data breach. Personal phones may also have unsafe apps on them, so they could get malware. However, staff could reply faster when they aren't at the desk.

Why: two points are applied and developed. The last point is about convenience rather than security, so the discussion isn't fully balanced.

Strong: Level 3, about 6 marks

Patient details are sensitive, so if a receptionist's phone is lost and isn't locked, anyone who finds it could read them. Bright Smiles would then have a personal data breach to deal with. Personal phones are also harder to control, because Bright Smiles can't easily stop staff installing unsafe apps or delaying updates, which raises the risk of malware reaching work email. On the other hand, these risks can be reduced. Mobile device management could require a passcode and allow work data to be wiped remotely, and multi-factor authentication would protect the account if a password were stolen. However, these controls only work if staff agree to them on their own phones, so Bright Smiles would need a clear policy.

Why: it explains the consequences for this business and looks at the issue from more than one side. A 'discuss' answer doesn't need a final conclusion.
